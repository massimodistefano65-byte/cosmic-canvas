*"After a disaster, nothing ever goes back exactly as it was before. But life continues to seek a balance."*



**Description of the work**

*Cellular Reorganization After the Disaster* stems from the depiction of a state following a rupture – an event capable of profoundly altering a pre-existing balance. The ‘disaster’ evoked by the title need not necessarily be interpreted as a concrete event: it may represent a crisis, a transformation or a rupture that radically alters the structure of what existed before.

The surface of the work appears as a disrupted organic landscape, composed of fragments, cavities, reliefs and forms that seem to belong to a living matter. What might initially appear to be a destroyed structure gradually reveals a different process: the cells are not simply scattered, but seem to be seeking a new arrangement.

It is precisely this **reorganisation** that lies at the heart of the work. Once the previous equilibrium has been disrupted, each element seems to react to the transformation by attempting to reassemble a viable structure. It is therefore not a simple reconstruction, but a process in which the material seeks to rediscover its own coherence, starting from the new conditions created by the disaster.

The three-dimensional nature of the surface accentuates this perception. The polystyrene, modelled and transformed, takes on an almost biological quality: it is as though one were observing an organism from within, a cellular tissue in which each element reacts to the others and to changes in the surrounding environment. The irregularities, depressions and raised areas thus become traces of a transformation still in progress.

The work prompts a broader reflection on life’s capacity to react to destruction. When a system is disrupted, it does not necessarily disappear: it can change, adapt and seek a new equilibrium. Cellular reorganisation thus becomes a metaphor for the human condition, in which even after a profound rupture, a drive towards reconstruction persists.

From this perspective, disaster does not necessarily signify the end. It is the moment when the previous order is lost and something must find a new configuration. Life does not simply restore what was: **it reorganises itself**.

*Cellular Reorganisation After the Disaster* thus embodies an essential and almost primordial idea: even when everything seems compromised, something continues to seek a form. Not necessarily the same form as before, but a form capable of re-establishing a balance.



**Technique and materials**

The work is created using mixed media on polystyrene, which has been worked and modelled to achieve a highly textured, three-dimensional surface. The structure combines reliefs, cavities, fragmentations and layers that contribute to creating the organic, almost cellular appearance of the composition.
